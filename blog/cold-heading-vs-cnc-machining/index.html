Cold Heading vs CNC Machining: Which Process Is Right for Your Fasteners?

Cold heading and CNC machining produce fasteners very differently —?in cost, speed, strength, and geometry limits. Here is a practical comparison to help you choose the right process for your part.

2026-08-06 7 min read
Home/Blog/Article

The short answer

If your part is a standard or slightly modified fastener with high volume and simple geometry, cold heading is almost always cheaper and faster. If your part has complex shapes, tight tolerances, internal features, or low volume, CNC machining is the better fit. Many projects —?including ours at Taily Metal —?use both processes on the same part: cold heading for the main body, then CNC for secondary features.

What is cold heading?

Cold heading (cold forming) takes wire rod and rams it into a die at room temperature to form the fastener head, body, and other features in one or several blows. No cutting, no heat, no material waste —?the metal is pushed, not removed.

What is CNC machining?

CNC machining removes material from bar stock with rotating cutting tools (turning, milling, drilling). It is subtractive: chips are cut away until the final shape remains. That flexibility is its superpower —?any geometry you can draw, a CNC machine can cut.

Cost and volume

Cold heading shines at high volume: once tooling is set up, parts are produced at hundreds per minute with almost zero material loss. CNC machining has lower setup cost but higher per-part cost —?it only removes material, and the removed material is wasted. As a rule of thumb, cold heading wins at volumes above a few tens of thousands of pieces per year; CNC wins for prototypes and low volumes.

Strength and material grain

Cold forming has a hidden advantage: the metal's grain structure follows the part shape, which strengthens the fastener and improves fatigue resistance. Machined parts keep the straight grain of the original bar, and cutting can leave micro-tears on the surface. For high-strength fasteners (8.8, 10.9, 12.9 grades), cold heading plus heat treatment is the standard production route.

Geometry limits

Cold heading is limited to forms that can be pushed into a die —?heads, shoulders, flanges, undercuts. It cannot make deep holes, fine internal threads, or shapes that would tear the material. CNC machining has no such limits: threads, grooves, cross-holes, hex pockets, and complex contours are all routine.

Material range

Both processes work with carbon steel, stainless steel, alloy steel, brass, and aluminum. Very hard or brittle alloys are easier to machine than to cold form; very soft, ductile materials are ideal for cold heading.

Hybrid approach —?the practical answer

At Taily Metal we combine the two: cold heading for the bulk geometry, then CNC for thread reliefs, cross-drilled holes, flats, and other secondary features.
